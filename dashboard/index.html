How one MoE layer runs

Blue arrows carry predictions, grey ones carry expert weights. Step through the seven stages, or click a stage below.

  1. The router picks this layer's experts for every sequence in the batch, replayed from the real trace.
  2. That choice goes to the predictor as history. It only ever learns from what the router really did.
  3. The predictor estimates how likely each expert is at layers l+1 to l+4 and hands that to the prefetcher.
  4. The prefetcher requests likely experts early, capped by link time and by an expected-value gate.
  5. The link copies weights from host DRAM into HBM. Demand misses preempt prefetches at 16 MB chunks.
  6. The same predictions decide what the HBM cache evicts: the expert least likely needed in the next 4 tokens.
  7. Experts run on weights already in HBM; any still arriving run as soon as they land. The next layer starts at step 1.

How these numbers were made
  • Main results: our trace-driven decode timeline on a simulated A100-80GB in FP16, with all routed experts in host DRAM. Every latency and energy cost comes from LLMSimulator; with caching and prefetching off, the timeline reproduces LLMSimulator's own numbers.
  • Routing: real Hugging Face traces for Mixtral-8x7B, Llama-4 Maverick and DeepSeek-R1. Grok-1 has no public trace; its routing is synthetic, fitted to Mixtral.
  • Full design: temporal, cross-layer and popularity predictors, calibrated and combined; adaptive prefetch budget; lookahead up to 4 layers with demand-first link scheduling; predicted-need eviction; miss overlap; expected-value gate.
  • H100: the real Mixtral-8x7B on an H100 NVL over PCIe 5.0. "Measured costs" means the simulator used link and compute costs measured on that card.
  • Data: data.js is generated from the repository's result CSVs by dashboard/build_data.py.
